What a p-value actually tells you (and what it doesn't)

Explainer · 7 minute read

Every week a headline announces that a study "found a significant link" between two things. Somewhere in that study sits a small number, often written as p < 0.05, doing a lot of quiet work. The p-value is one of the most used and most misunderstood numbers in science. Understanding it takes one idea, one warning and a little imagination.

Start by assuming nothing is going on

Imagine testing whether a new fertiliser makes tomatoes heavier. You grow one bed with it and one without, and the fertilised tomatoes come out a little heavier on average. Is that the fertiliser, or just luck? Tomatoes vary anyway, so two beds will never weigh exactly the same.

A test starts from a deliberately boring assumption, called the null hypothesis: the fertiliser does nothing, and any difference is chance. Then it asks a precise question. If the fertiliser really did nothing, how often would chance alone produce a difference at least as big as the one we saw? That probability is the p-value. A p-value of 0.03 means that in a world where the fertiliser is useless, a gap this large or larger would turn up in about three experiments out of a hundred.

The warning: it is not the chance you are wrong

The most common mistake is to read p = 0.03 as "there is a 3 percent chance the fertiliser doesn't work". That is backwards. The p-value is calculated by assuming the null hypothesis is true, so it cannot also tell you how likely that hypothesis is. It measures how surprising your data would be in a boring world, not how likely the boring world is.

The difference matters. If a claim was unlikely to begin with, such as a sugar pill curing a serious illness, a small p-value from one study is far weaker evidence than it sounds, because a surprising fluke is still more believable than a miracle. Judging how likely a hypothesis is needs more than one number: earlier evidence, the size of the study and a sensible mechanism all count.

Why 0.05, and why it is not magic

Many fields call a result "statistically significant" when p falls below 0.05. There is nothing special about that line. It is a habit that grew out of early twentieth century textbooks, a convenient round number that stuck. A result with p = 0.049 is not meaningfully different from one with p = 0.051, yet the first is often celebrated and the second ignored. In 2016 the American Statistical Association's statement on p-values warned against exactly this kind of bright-line thinking, and asked researchers to report the full picture instead of a yes or no.

Significant is not the same as important

"Significant" in statistics only means "unlikely to be pure chance". It says nothing about whether the effect is big enough to care about. With a huge sample, a fertiliser that adds two grams to a two-hundred-gram tomato can produce a tiny p-value: the effect is real, but no gardener would notice it. With a small sample, a genuinely large effect can fail to reach significance because there is too much noise to be sure.

That is why careful studies report the effect size, how big the difference is, together with a range of plausible values, and not just whether p crossed a line. The question "is there an effect?" is less useful than "how big is the effect, and how sure are we?"

Test enough things and something will "work"

If you run one test on a useless fertiliser, you have about a one in twenty chance of getting p < 0.05 by luck. Now imagine testing it on tomatoes, peppers, beans, lettuce and twenty other crops. Even if it does nothing at all, it would be surprising if none of them came out "significant". Report only that one crop, and you have a headline built on a coin toss.

This is the multiple comparisons problem, and it has a darker cousin: trying different analyses, subgroups or stopping points until something dips below 0.05, sometimes called p-hacking. Good practice is to decide the analysis before seeing the data, report every test you ran, and adjust the threshold when many tests are made at once.

How to read the next headline

When you see "a significant link", ask four quick questions. Significant compared with what boring assumption? How big is the effect in plain units? How many things did they test? And was the claim plausible before this study? A p-value is a useful smoke alarm: it tells you the data would be surprising if nothing were happening. It does not tell you where the fire is, how big it is, or whether someone was waving a match under the detector.
