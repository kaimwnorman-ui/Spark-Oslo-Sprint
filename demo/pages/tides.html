Why the sea breathes: how tides really work

A plain-language guide · 7 minute read

Stand on almost any beach for a few hours and the sea will move. The water creeps up the sand, pauses, and slides back out, twice a day, every day. It looks like breathing. It has nothing to do with wind or waves: the whole ocean is being reshaped by the Moon and the Sun, and the coast you are standing on decides how dramatic the result looks.

It is not the pull, it is the difference in pull

Most people learn that "the Moon pulls the water". That is close, but it misses the clever part. The Moon pulls on everything on Earth, rocks and oceans alike, and its gravity gets weaker with distance. The ocean on the side of Earth facing the Moon is about 6,400 kilometres closer to it than the centre of the planet, so it is pulled a little harder than the centre. The ocean on the far side is pulled a little less than the centre. It is this difference across the planet, called the tidal force, that matters. A uniform pull would simply move the whole Earth; an uneven pull stretches it.

The Sun does the same thing. It is far more massive than the Moon, but it is also about 390 times farther away, and the stretching effect fades very quickly with distance. The result is that the Sun's tide is a bit less than half as strong as the Moon's.

Two bulges, so two high tides

Stretch a ball of water and it does not grow one bump, it grows two. One bulge points toward the Moon, where the water is pulled ahead of the planet. The other points away from the Moon, on the far side, where the water is pulled less than the solid Earth and is, in effect, left behind. Between the bulges the ocean is a little lower.

Now spin the planet. Earth turns once a day underneath this stretched shape, so a beach is carried through one bulge, then a low, then the other bulge, then another low. That is why most coasts see two high tides and two low tides each day, not one. The bulges are not water rushing around the globe; it is the ground that is moving through them.

Why high tide is about 50 minutes later every day

If you check a tide table, high tide is never at the same time two days running. While Earth spins, the Moon is also travelling along its orbit, in the same direction Earth turns. After one full spin, the Moon has moved on by about 13 degrees, so Earth has to keep turning for roughly another 50 minutes before your beach faces it again. A "tidal day" is therefore about 24 hours and 50 minutes long, and each high tide arrives about 25 minutes after half of that. The NASA page on the Moon and tides has a good animation of the Moon running ahead.

Spring tides and neap tides

Because the Sun raises its own, smaller tide, the two effects add up or work against each other. At new moon and full moon, the Sun, the Moon and Earth sit in a line, the two bulges stack, and the difference between high and low water is at its largest. These are called spring tides. The name has nothing to do with the season: it comes from an old word meaning to leap or rise, and they happen all year round, about every two weeks.

A week later, at the quarter moons, the Sun and the Moon pull at right angles. The Sun's tide partly fills in the Moon's lows and shaves its highs, so the range shrinks. These gentler tides are neap tides. Fishers and sailors plan around this two-week rhythm, which is why many tide tables print the moon phase next to the times.

The coastline decides the size

Out in the open ocean, the tidal bulge is surprisingly modest, often well under a metre. What turns it into a spectacle is the shape of the coast and the seafloor. A wide bay that narrows into a funnel squeezes the incoming water upward. If the bay's own natural slosh, the time water takes to rock from one end to the other and back, happens to match the roughly twelve-and-a-half-hour tide, each tide gives the sloshing another push, like pushing a child on a swing at just the right moment.

The Bay of Fundy in Canada has both: a funnel shape and a near-perfect match, and its tides rise and fall by up to 16 metres. The Mediterranean, by contrast, connects to the Atlantic only through the narrow Strait of Gibraltar, so very little tidal water gets in and many of its beaches barely notice the tide at all. Some coasts, because of how their basins resonate, even get just one high tide a day. So a big tide does not mean a place is closer to the Moon; it means the local shape of the sea amplifies the same gentle push.

Reading the sea yourself

Put these pieces together and a tide table stops being a mystery. Two highs a day come from the two bulges. Each day they slip later because the Moon moves on. Every two weeks they swell and fade with the moon phase. And the local coastline sets the scale.
