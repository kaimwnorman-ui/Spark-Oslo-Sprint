How a solar panel turns sunlight into electricity

Workbench guide · 7 minute read

A solar panel has no moving parts, makes no noise and burns nothing, yet on a bright day a single rooftop panel can run a fridge. Inside that dark glass rectangle, light is pushing electrons around. Here is what actually happens, step by step, from a photon leaving the Sun to a kettle boiling in your kitchen.

Light knocks electrons loose

Sunlight arrives as tiny packets of energy called photons. Most panels are made of thin slices of silicon, and in silicon each atom holds its outer electrons in shared bonds with its neighbours. When a photon with enough energy hits one of those bonds, it can knock an electron free, leaving behind a gap called a hole. The freed electron can now wander through the crystal. This is the photovoltaic effect: light directly creating mobile electric charges in a material.

On its own, though, that is not electricity you can use. A loose electron soon finds a hole and falls back in, and its energy turns into a little heat. To get a current, something has to push the freed electrons away from the holes before they meet again.

A built-in slope pushes them one way

That push comes from how the silicon is prepared. One layer is mixed with a trace of phosphorus, which gives it spare electrons; this is the n-type layer. The other is mixed with boron, which leaves it short of electrons; this is the p-type layer. Where the two layers meet, at the p–n junction, some electrons drift across and settle, building up a permanent electric field.

You can picture that field as a slope inside the cell. When light frees an electron near the junction, the slope sweeps it towards the n-type side and sweeps the hole the other way. Electrons pile up on one side, so connect a wire from the front of the cell to the back and they flow round through it, doing work on the way. A single solar cell gives only about half a volt, so a panel wires dozens of cells in a row to add their voltages together.

From panel power to house power

The current from the panel always flows in one direction: it is direct current, like a battery's. The sockets in a house use alternating current, which reverses direction many times a second. So every home system includes a box called an inverter, which chops the steady flow into the alternating rhythm the grid and your appliances expect. Modern inverters also keep adjusting the load to squeeze the most power out of the panels as the light changes through the day.

Why panels waste most of the light

A typical rooftop panel converts around a fifth of the sunlight that hits it into electricity. The rest is not lost through sloppy engineering; much of it is physics. Silicon needs each photon to carry at least a certain amount of energy to free an electron. Red and infrared photons below that amount pass through or warm the panel and do nothing useful. Blue photons carry far more than enough, but an electron can only use the minimum; the extra is shed as heat within a split second. Because of this, a single layer of silicon can never convert more than about a third of sunlight, however perfectly it is made.

Cold, bright and facing the Sun

Two practical facts surprise people. First, panels work better cold. Heat makes the atoms in the crystal jiggle more, which lowers the voltage each cell can provide, so on a scorching day a panel can deliver noticeably less than on a cool, clear spring morning. Second, angle matters as much as brightness. A panel facing the Sun squarely catches the most photons; tilt it away and the same beam is spread over more glass, so each part receives less. That is why installers point panels towards the equator at a tilt that depends on how far north or south you live.

Putting it together

Light frees electrons, the junction's built-in field sends them one way, a wire lets them flow round, and an inverter turns that steady flow into household current. Physics caps how much of the light can be used, heat trims it further, and pointing the panel well wins some back.
