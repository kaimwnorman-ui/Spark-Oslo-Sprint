How does HTTPS actually keep my connection private? I see the padlock but I don't know what it does.

AI

Good question: the padlock stands for a few separate protections working together. HTTPS is ordinary web traffic (HTTP) sent through a protective layer called TLS. Here is what that layer does, roughly in the order it happens when your browser opens a site.

1. Everything is scrambled in transit

Without HTTPS, the requests and pages travelling between your browser and the website are plain text. Anyone on the path, such as the café Wi-Fi, your internet provider or a router along the way, could read them, including passwords and messages. With HTTPS, both ends encrypt the data, so someone in the middle only sees scrambled bytes. They can tell that you are exchanging data, and roughly how much, but not what it says.

2. The two sides agree on a secret without ever sending it

Encryption needs a shared key, but your browser and the server have never met. Sending the key openly would defeat the purpose. So the connection starts with a short conversation called the handshake. Using clever mathematics (a key exchange), each side contributes a random value and both compute the same session key, while an eavesdropper who sees the whole conversation still cannot work it out. That session key is then used for fast symmetric encryption of everything else. A new key is made for each connection, so even if one session were cracked later, others would stay safe.

3. A certificate proves you are talking to the real site

Secrecy is useless if you are secretly talking to an impostor. During the handshake, the server shows a certificate: a document that says "this key belongs to example.com", digitally signed by a certificate authority. Your browser or operating system ships with a list of authorities it trusts and checks the signature chain back to one of them. If the name does not match or the signature is wrong, you get a full-page warning instead of a padlock. This is what stops someone on the café network from pretending to be your bank.

4. Tampering is detected

Every piece of encrypted data also carries a kind of tamper seal. If anyone changes even one bit along the way, the check fails and the connection is dropped. That means nobody can quietly inject ads, malware or altered numbers into the page you receive.

What HTTPS does not hide

HTTPS protects the content, not the fact that you visited. The name of the site you connect to is usually still visible to your network, and the site itself of course sees everything you send it. The padlock also says nothing about whether a site is honest: a scam site can have a perfectly valid certificate. It means "this connection is private and goes to whoever owns this domain", not "this domain is trustworthy".
